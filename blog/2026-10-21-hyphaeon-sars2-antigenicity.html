How SARS-CoV-2 learned to hide from our naïve B cells—and how HyphAeon sees it in the tree

21 Oct 2026, by Sergei · 3 min read · Target Preprint: bioRxiv (2026.10.15.759476) · HyphAeon Preprint: Forthcoming
Key Takeaways

Standard viral selection scans average over the entire family tree, mistaking pandemic sweeps that reach 100% prevalence for conserved sites and missing how escape mutations rewire the protein fold. Pairing Catherine Jacob-Dolan, Jim Boonyaratanakornkit, and Jesse Bloom’s new naïve human B-cell measurements with HyphAeon—our 1.9-million-parameter phylogenetic foundation model—shows that mutations erasing human germline B-cell recognition drove the fastest selective sweeps of the pandemic before shifting evolution to a cross-domain NTD clamp.

Whenever a new preprint from Jesse Bloom’s lab drops on bioRxiv, I stop what I am doing and read it. Last Friday’s paper by Catherine Jacob-Dolan, Jim Boonyaratanakornkit, and Jesse Bloom (Jacob-Dolan et al., 2026) is a classic of the genre: a clean, experimentally decisive study that exposes an evolutionary mechanism hiding in plain sight.

We know SARS-CoV-2 evolves to escape neutralizing antibodies from prior exposures. Catherine, Jim, and Jesse asked a deeper question: has the receptor-binding domain (RBD) also evolved to become inherently less visible to unmutated, naïve human B cells? Using human blood cells frozen between 2003 and 2007—long before COVID-19—they found that 3-fold fewer naïve human B cells recognize the 2024 KP.2 RBD ($0.0040\%$) than Wuhan-Hu-1 ($0.011\%$). Even better, naïve mouse B cells showed zero decrease ($0.013\%$ across all variants), proving that SARS-CoV-2 specifically shaved off surface motifs targeted by the human germline antibody repertoire (Shrock et al., 2023).

Reading their paper over coffee, I realized their data was the ideal testbed for HyphAeon—a phylogenetic foundation model we built over the past year. What happens when we project their nine RBD constructs and 31 variant sites onto 78 months of SARS-CoV-2 Spike evolution?

Let's start with the sharpest contrast in their paper. In Figure 2B, the authors test five famous single mutations in the Wuhan-Hu-1 RBD: E484K, E484A, K417N, L452R, and F486P. All five escape mature Wuhan-Hu-1 serum antibodies. Yet only two—E484A ($2.34\times$ drop, $p_{\mathrm{adj}} = 0.007$) and F486P ($1.90\times$ drop, $p_{\mathrm{adj}} = 0.045$)—significantly reduce naïve human B-cell recognition, while E484K at the exact same residue leaves naïve B-cell binding intact ($0.010\%$ vs. $0.011\%$, $p_{\mathrm{adj}} = 0.58$). To see whether germline stealth shaped how these mutations spread in nature, we tracked their selective trajectories across the global SARS-CoV-2 tree (Fig. 1).

Before looking at the numbers, how does HyphAeon measure selection across a tree? Most protein language models read whole sequences across flat alignments without a tree, using hundreds of millions of parameters to memorize protein families. HyphAeon is a 1.9-million-parameter geometric transformer trained on $17{,}186$ mammalian alignments ($742$ species, $9.77\text{M}$ codons) scored by HyPhy MEME. It evaluates one codon column at a time alongside a 4D coordinate map of branch distances, routing silent ($dS$) and replacement ($dN$) changes through separate channels.

Because a forward pass takes milliseconds and assigns an attention weight $\hat{a}_{s,m}$ to every mutated branch, differentiating site attribution over time measures instantaneous selection velocity:

$$v_s(t) = \max\!\left(0,\, \frac{d}{dt}\hat{a}_s(t)\right)$$

Why differentiate over time? Standard whole-tree scans suffer from post-fixation dilution: once F486P sweeps to $100\%$, every descendant inherits that proline, and static models mistake those thousands of identical branches for purifying conservation. Selection velocity $v_s(t)$ peaks as a variant takes off—warning of major sweeps 2.5 to 9.8 months before $80\%$ global dominance—and drops to zero at fixation. Meanwhile, outer products of branch attributions across sites extract epistatic co-selection ($\mathrm{CESI}_{ij}$) between co-evolving residues.

Running HyphAeon across $3{,}839$ dated Spike genomes ($1{,}863$ haplotypes, Dec 2019–mid 2026) and comparing against Jacob-Dolan, Boonyaratanakornkit, and Bloom’s 31 RBD sites (their Fig. 1B) yields three clean findings (Fig. 2):

  • Germline-stealth escape beats germline-visible escape by $4.2\times$: In 2021, Beta and Gamma introduced E484K on the Wuhan-Hu-1 background. Because E484K still binds naïve human B cells ($0.010\%$), primary infections elicited potent anti-K484 germline antibodies; its velocity stalled ($v_{\max} = 0.68 \times 10^{-3}$, selective integral $\mathrm{AUC} = 0.167 \times 10^{-3}$), peaking at $15.7\%$ before vanishing in 2022 (Fig. 1B). When Omicron introduced E484A ($2.34\times$ naïve B-cell drop), it escaped serum without recruiting de novo B cells against A484, generating a $4.23\times$ larger velocity integral ($\mathrm{AUC} = 0.706 \times 10^{-3}$) and sweeping to $98.8\%$ fixation. Likewise, F486P ($1.90\times$ B-cell drop) reached the highest velocity of all tested mutants ($v_{\max} = 2.92 \times 10^{-3}$, $4.6\times$ above L452R and $7.1\times$ above K417N).
  • Rescuing 76% of RBD sweeps—and solving the KP.2 A484K puzzle: Across the 31 RBD sites in Figure 1B, HyphAeon velocity confirms 29 of 31 (93.5%) as episodic sweeps ($p_{\mathrm{perm}} \le 0.034$) versus 12 of 192 (6.2%) across the rest of the RBD ($\mathrm{OR} = 217.5, p = 1.5 \times 10^{-24}$), rescuing 22 of 29 sweeps (75.9%)—including F486P and K417N—missed by static scans (Fig. 2A). Look closely at site 484 in Figure 1B: KP.2 reverted from A484 back to K484, yet its naïve B-cell binding dropped 1.5-fold below XBB.1.5 ($0.0061\% \to 0.0040\%$). Why? Unlike 2021 E484K ($0\%$ co-occurrence), 2024–2026 A484K ($n = 2{,}787$ genomes) is 100% co-selected with the KP.2 codon 455/456/452/450 ridge hub ($\mathrm{CESI} = 6.89\text{–}8.93, q < 10^{-300}$; Dadonaite et al., 2024) and K356T ($100\%$ prevalence, $\sum\mathrm{CESI} = 62.4$), which installs an N354 glycan shield over the RBD core.
  • From RBD germline loss to NTD conformational clamping: To address the authors’ third study limitation—that monomeric RBD probes miss other Spike domains and "RBD-down" conformational shifts—we tracked all 129 full-Spike sweeps (Fig. 2B). Selection velocity peaked first in the RBM around 2022.20 (E484A, F486P), then shifted to the N-terminal domain (NTD, 39 sweeps, median peak 2023.03, highest mean velocity $1.14 \times 10^{-3}$). Fully 66.1% (312 / 472) of RBD epistatic pairs connect to other domains, including 186 NTD–RBD pairs (led by S50L–L455S, $\mathrm{CESI} = 12.41$) that pack against the neighboring protomer’s closed RBD. Once exposed RBD germline motifs were depleted, SARS-CoV-2 co-evolved an NTD latch to hide the remaining RBD epitopes in the "down" state.

This is why pairing quantitative immunology from Catherine Jacob-Dolan, Jim Boonyaratanakornkit, and Jesse Bloom with millisecond phylogenetic foundation models is so much fun: wet-lab flow cytometry tells us what changed in human B-cell recognition, and HyphAeon’s velocity and epistasis operators show how natural selection wired those changes together across the viral tree.
