How HIV-TRACE learned to stop worrying and love the molecular clock

16 Oct 2026, by Sergei · 3 min read · Preprint: Forthcoming
Key Takeaways

Standard HIV-1 molecular surveillance uses fixed genetic-distance cutoffs (0.5% or 1.5%) that ignore when sequences were sampled—severing delayed transmission links while chaining unlinked patients and lab errors into giant "megaclusters." By embedding pairwise distances into an explicit coalescent molecular clock, TRACE-5.0 nearly doubles resource-normalized case yield, cuts false-alarm clusters by 93%, and estimates total undiagnosed epidemic size in seconds inside a local web browser.

For the past decade, routine HIV-1 molecular surveillance in municipal and national health departments has relied on a remarkably simple recipe (Kosakovsky Pond et al., 2018): take the pol gene sequences collected during standard drug-resistance testing, compute all pairwise Tamura–Nei (TN93) genetic distances, and connect any two patients whose sequences differ by less than a fixed threshold—typically $1.5\%$, or $0.5\%$ for US federal priority monitoring.

Pairwise distance graphs became the workhorse of public health surveillance because they are fast, intuitive, and tree-free. Yet a static threshold has a glaring blind spot: it treats two viral sequences sampled on the same afternoon identically to two sequences sampled four years apart.

In a new study with Steven Weaver, Darren Martin, Taylor Paisie, Daria Chalkova, Brittany Rife Magalis, Simon Frost, and Joel Wertheim (TRACE-5.0: Dynamic Transmission Surveillance, or How HIV-TRACE Learned to Stop Worrying and Love the Molecular Clock), we show how replacing flat distance cutoffs with an explicit, tree-free molecular clock resolves the worst pathologies of molecular surveillance across 10,259 patients in four global cohorts.

What goes wrong when you draw a flat horizontal cutoff across a living epidemic? Two opposite failures happen at once (Fig. 1C):

  • Tight cutoffs ($0.5\%$) sever delayed diagnoses: Even in a direct transmission pair, HIV-1 accumulates ~4 ancestral donor polymorphisms plus forward mutations at $\mu \approx 2.0 \times 10^{-3}\text{ subs/site/year}$. Under our coalescent model, the probability that a true transmission pair stays below $0.5\%$ drops from $73.8\%$ at simultaneous sampling ($\Delta T = 0$) to $34.6\%$ at two years and $11.2\%$ at four years. In the Washington, DC cohort ($N = 1{,}658$, Pérez-Losada et al., 2017), a strict $0.5\%$ ceiling severs 38.8% (19/49) of clock-supported transmission dyads—and collapses prospective case recall to just 8.9%.
  • Lenient cutoffs ($1.5\%$) chain unlinked patients and lab errors into "megaclusters": Conversely, two sequences sampled four years apart with zero mutations ($d = 0\%$, $\Delta T = 4.03\text{ yr}$) cannot represent active forward replication—they are unmutated specimen duplicates, clinic re-accessions, or HXB2 lab carryover ("Wo Fat" artifacts). A static threshold clusters them with maximum confidence, chaining background lineages into sprawling hairballs: a 90-patient megacluster in DC (Fig. 1A), a 332-patient HXB2-nucleated cluster in Middle Tennessee (Dennis et al., 2018), a 2,896-patient component (70.4% of clustered cases!) across Japan (Shiino et al., 2020), and 91.2% of a founder-compressed CRF07_BC cohort in Shenzhen (Zhao et al., 2016).

How do we bring molecular-clock realism to surveillance registries of 5,000+ patients without waiting 48 hours for Bayesian MCMC trees in BEAST to converge? TRACE-5.0 operates across three mathematical scales—all in closed form or fast spectral algebra:

  1. Dyadic Scale — DANNO (Dynamic Ancestral Negative-binomial Network Odds): For every pair of sequences separated by $K = d \cdot L$ substitutions and $\Delta T = |t_i - t_j|$ years, DANNO integrates over exponentially distributed donor coalescent depth ($\bar{\tau}$) and forward Poisson evolution ($\mu$) in closed form via the regularized upper incomplete gamma function $Q(s,x) = \Gamma(s,x)/\Gamma(s)$: $$P(K \mid \Delta T, H_{\text{trans}}) = \frac{\exp\!\left(\frac{\Delta T}{2\bar{\tau}}\right)}{\bar{\tau}} \left[ \frac{(2\mu L)^K}{(2\mu L + \bar{\tau}^{-1})^{K+1}} \right] Q\!\left(K + 1, \, \frac{2\mu L + \bar{\tau}^{-1}}{2}\Delta T\right)$$ We compare $H_{\text{trans}}$ against a self-calibrated Negative-Binomial/triangular background null ($H_{\text{null}}$), hard-zero any pair that fails a Poisson molecular-clock adequacy test ($p_{\text{adeq}} < 0.05 \implies \mathbf{BF}=0$), and certify edges via Bayesian False Discovery Rate control ($q \le 0.10$ or $0.05$).
  2. Community Scale — STEVE (Spectral Temporal Evolutionary Velocity Engine): To break apart sprawling single-linkage components without building a tree, STEVE recursively bisects sequence affinity graphs using Fiedler eigenvectors (Cheeger cuts) and fits Bartlett-adjusted root-to-tip regressions with exact Fieller confidence bounds. That separates Tier 1 Active Outbreaks (high evolutionary velocity, median $\mu = 15.2 \times 10^{-3}\text{ sub/site/yr}$ in DC, driven by rapid acute replication before purifying selection prunes transient variants) and Tier 2 Emergent Seeds from Tier 3 Chronic/Endemic Reservoirs ($\mu \le 5 \times 10^{-4}$ or unidentifiable clocks $g \ge 1$).
  3. Macro-Epidemic Scale — CHIN (Cluster-to-Host Incidence and Network): Sampling an infected person scales linearly with surveillance coverage ($\mathbb{E}[n] = \rho N_{\mathrm{act}}$), but sampling both partners of a transmission link scales quadratically ($\mathbb{E}[E_{\mathrm{obs}}] \propto \rho^2$, just like the birthday paradox). By inverting $\hat{\theta} = 2E_{\mathrm{obs}}/n = \widehat{\rho R_0 (1+\phi)}$ across priors for $R_0$, superspreading overdispersion $k$, and partner-tracing enrichment $\phi$, CHIN estimates total active epidemic size directly from network topology.

To test whether this actually helps public health teams allocate limited field resources, we ran strict retrospective temporal-holdout validations across Washington, DC, Middle Tennessee, Japan, and Shenzhen—calibrating strictly on historical cases ($t \le T_{\mathrm{censor}}$) and measuring who linked to future cases over the next 3.3 years (Fig. 2):

  • Nearly doubling patient-level case yield (DANNO): In Washington, DC ($T_{\mathrm{censor}} = 2012.50$), targeting acute DANNO clusters ($q \le 0.05, \Delta T \le 2\text{ yr}$) focused partner services on just 54 historical patients (a 61.4% reduction in workload vs. 140 under static $1.5\%$) while capturing 43 follow-up cases—boosting yield by 99.0% ($0.400 \to 0.796$ follow-up cases per targeted patient; Fig. 2D2) and retaining 76.8–100% recall across all four global cohorts without manual threshold tuning.
  • Cutting false-alarm clusters by 92.9% (STEVE): Static $1.5\%$ clustering flagged 41 DC clusters, producing 28 false alarms (31.7% precision). Standard size-normalized growth ($\Delta N / \sqrt{N} \ge 0.70$) reached 36.4% precision (21.2% at 12 months), with over half its follow-up cases absorbed passively by a single 49-person background hairball (Fig. 2C). STEVE prioritized 12 high-velocity clusters, 10 of which expanded (83.3% precision, 58.3% within 12 months, and just 2 false alarms; Fisher $\text{OR} = 8.75, p = 0.0074$).
  • Recovering true municipal HIV prevalence from network collisions (CHIN): Across all four registries, CHIN's quadratic collision inversion recovered independent public health benchmarks—estimating $\hat{N}_{\mathrm{act}} = 19{,}700$ ($[13{,}100, 29{,}500]$ at $\hat{\rho} = 8.4\%$ coverage) in DC vs. 16,000–18,000 total living HIV cases (Fig. 3C); $\hat{N}_{\mathrm{act}} = 17{,}409$ in Middle Tennessee vs. 13,000–16,500; $\hat{N}_{\mathrm{act}} = 14{,}114$ in Japan vs. 16,000–22,000; and $\hat{N}_{\mathrm{act}} = 611$ in Shenzhen vs. the 600–900 diagnosed CRF07_BC catchment.

Because clinical viral genotypes are protected health records that cannot be uploaded to external cloud servers, we wrote TRACE-5.0 in pure Rust and compiled it to WebAssembly (trace50-wasm). A municipal epidemiologist can drop 5,000+ sequences into a web browser and run the entire DANNO + STEVE + CHIN pipeline in under 10 seconds—completely offline, with zero data ever leaving the health department firewall.
