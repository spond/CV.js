Methods & Reticulate Evolution

Clean breaks: how RhizAeon finds recombination across whole genomes in seconds

24 Oct 2026, by Sergei · 4 min read · 60-sec slide deck · Preprint: bioRxiv 2026.10.19.760237
Key Takeaways

When viruses or bacteria swap DNA, a single family tree can no longer describe the alignment—yet tree-splitting tools are too slow for whole genomes, and fast triplet scanners count the same ancestral swap dozens of times. RhizAeon places reference sequences on a map, tracks where each genome points using continuous attention weights, and detects parent swaps as sharp force dipoles polished across multiple scales—scanning whole viral and bacterial genomes in seconds inside a web browser.

When two viruses infect the same cell—or a bacterium takes up DNA from a neighbor—the child genome is a mosaic: part comes from Parent A, and part comes from Parent B. Forcing that mosaic alignment onto a single family tree distorts branch lengths and creates false positives in $dN/dS$ selection scans (Murrell et al., 2012; Murrell et al., 2015).

Twenty years ago, David Posada, Simon Frost, and I built GARD to find where the tree changes along an alignment. GARD works well on single genes, but rebuilding trees across every candidate split is too slow for whole genomes: when Spyros Lytras, Darren Martin, David Robertson, and I analyzed 78 sarbecovirus genomes ($30{,}956$ letters) to trace the origins of SARS-CoV-2 (Lytras et al., 2022), we had to drop 60 genomes just to finish one GARD run in 4.5 days of cluster time.

In a new preprint with Danielle Callan, Steven Weaver, Hannah Verdonk, Jordan Zehr, Spyros Lytras, Anton Nekrutenko, Taylor Paisie, and Darren Martin (GitHub, browser app, Zenodo), we introduce RhizAeon—a method that maps recombinant DNA in linear $\mathcal{O}(NKL)$ time without building trees in windows or testing sequence triplets.

Why not use a fast triplet scanner such as 3SEQ or RDP5? Triplet scanners check three sequences at a time ($\binom{N}{3}$ triplets) to see if Sequence C matches Parent A on the left and Parent B on the right. Testing each triplet by itself creates three problems on large alignments (Fig. 1):

  • Counting one ancestral swap many times: When one swap happens in an ancestor that leaves 10 descendants, a triplet scanner tests every descendant against every pair of cousins—reporting $14.37 \pm 7.27$ separate events for one historical swap (Fig. 1B).
  • Mistaking fast mutation rates for hybrids: When one branch mutates faster than its neighbors, its distance to other sequences jumps even though no DNA was exchanged.
  • Missing unsampled ("ghost") parents and three-parent mosaics: Triplet tests assume both parents are in your sample and that every hybrid has only two parents. They fail when the donor is extinct or unsampled (Fig. 1C), or when three parents built one chromosome (Fig. 1D).

How does RhizAeon solve this in one linear pass? Instead of testing triplets one by one, RhizAeon runs a five-step stack that treats recombination as movement across a map:

  1. Reference Map and Center Drain (Root Sink): RhizAeon places $K$ diverse reference sequences as fixed landmarks $\mathbf{x}_k$ on a low-dimensional map (Gower, 1966), with a neutral center drain ($w_{i,0}$, the root sink) at the origin. When a branch mutates fast on its own, it moves away from all landmarks at once; that extra distance flows into the center drain instead of faking a parent swap.
  2. Attention Maps (Where Each Sequence Points): Moving left to right along the alignment ($u = 1 \dots L$), each sequence $i$ assigns soft attention weights $\mathbf{w}_i(u)$ to the $K$ landmarks (plus the center drain) to match its local position $\mathbf{r}_i(u)$ and genetic distances $\widetilde{D}_{ik}(u)$: $$\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w}_i(u)) = \frac{1}{2}\left\lVert \mathbf{r}_i(u) - \sum_{k=1}^{K} w_{i,k}(u)\,\mathbf{x}_k - \beta_i(u)\,\mathbf{d}_{\mathrm{ghost}} \right\rVert_2^2 + \gamma \sum_{k=1}^{K} w_{i,k}(u)\,\widetilde{D}_{ik}(u) - \tau \sum_{k=0}^{K} w_{i,k}(u)\ln w_{i,k}(u)$$ When sequence $i$ stays in one family branch, its attention map stays flat. If a segment comes from an unsampled "ghost" donor outside all landmarks, $\beta_i(u)$ lifts the sequence along an orthogonal ghost axis ($\mathbf{d}_{\mathrm{ghost}}$) at a right angle to the map.
  3. Force Dipoles (Turning Parent Swaps into Sharp Signals): At a breakpoint where sequence $i$ jumps from Parent A to Parent B, Parent A's weight drops while Parent B's weight rises by the same amount. Comparing the attention vector just left of site $u$ with the vector just right of site $u$ creates an equal-and-opposite force dipole ($\Delta \mathbf{w}_i(u) = \mathbf{w}_i^{+}(u) - \mathbf{w}_i^{-}(u)$): a push away from Parent A paired with an equal pull toward Parent B, which we test against a Brownian-bridge null model.
  4. Dyadic Polishing and Viterbi Decoding (Sharp Edges at Every Scale): Transferred DNA ranges from 80 letters in immune genes to 30,000 letters in bacteria. RhizAeon scans the force dipoles with a dyadic wavelet bank—matched filters whose widths double at each step ($2, 4, 8, 16, 32\dots$ informative sites)—to catch both tiny conversions and long transfers. A 5-state Viterbi decoder traces the parent path, and a local likelihood check polishes each breakpoint to the exact nucleotide.
  5. Iterative Scrubbing and Clade Merging: After confirming a tract, RhizAeon masks ("scrubs") that DNA out of the alignment and runs again to uncover older swaps underneath. Finally, if 10 descendants share the same breakpoints and parents, RhizAeon merges them into one ancestral event.

What does this stack find in real viral, bacterial, and primate genomes?

  • 78–83 whole sarbecovirus genomes in 2.6 seconds (vs. 4.5 days in GARD): On all 78 sarbecovirus genomes from Lytras et al. (2022) ($30{,}956\text{ nt}$) plus 5 Laotian BANAL bat viruses (Temmam et al., 2022), RhizAeon finishes in 2.6 seconds (Fig. 3). Force dipoles and dyadic polishing match all 21 whole-genome breakpoints from 4.5 days of GARD (median offset 99.0 nt; 9–12 letters at Spike junctions) and isolate the BANAL-20-52 / RaTG13 RBD swap ($p < 10^{-16}$).
  • A 2.22-megabase Streptococcus pneumoniae chromosome in 36.1 seconds: Across 240 multidrug-resistant S. pneumoniae PMEN1 chromosomes ($2{,}221{,}315\text{ bp}$, Croucher et al., 2011), RhizAeon maps 392 horizontal transfers in 36.1 seconds (~35× faster than Gubbins), pinpointing drug-resistance swaps in pbp2x, pbp1a, pbp2b, folA, folP, and the 23F $\to$ 19A capsule switch (cps).
  • 86–150 bp micro-conversions in 379 primate MHC Class I alleles in 1.1 seconds: Across 379 human and ape HLA-A/B/C alleles (Parham et al., 1995), fine-scale dyadic filters catch 103 short Exon 2/3 conversions (86–150 letters)—including the classic HLA-B*46:01 cassette spliced between HLA-B*15:01 and HLA-C*01:02 at positions 462–607 ($p = 2.8 \times 10^{-8}$).
