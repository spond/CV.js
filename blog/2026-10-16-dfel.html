Teaching codon models which way the compositional wind is blowing

16 Oct 2026, by Sergei · Preprint: bioRxiv 2026.10.14.759254

Codon substitution models should have a huge blind spot. Every time we run a standard $dN/dS$ scan to find sites under purifying or diversifying selection, we make a quiet pact with Felsenstein’s pulley principle: we assume that nucleotide composition is at stationary equilibrium across the entire phylogenetic tree. Under that assumption, $\text{AT} \to \text{GC}$ and $\text{GC} \to \text{AT}$ fluxes balance out on every branch, evolution is time-reversible, and the root of the tree doesn't matter.

Biology, of course, breaks this pact constantly. In birds and mammals, GC-biased gene conversion (gBGC) acts like a thumb on the scale during meiotic recombination repair, steadily pumping GC content up on microchromosomes and in recombination hotspots. In bacteria and mitochondria, asymmetric replication between leading and lagging DNA strands creates sharp nucleotide skews. In insects, translational selection reshapes third codon positions; in viruses, host deaminases like APOBEC3 and ADAR pepper specific clades with directional mutations.

I have wondered for a long time how badly these compositional tides distort site-level selection tests like Fixed Effects Likelihood (FEL), and how we might fix the problem without making inference grind to a halt on large trees.

In this post I will describe a new method, Directional Fixed Effects Likelihood (dFEL), that gives site-level codon models a sense of direction. The paper, A Sense of Direction: Detecting Natural Selection Across Non-Stationary Genomes, is now out on bioRxiv, and the method is implemented in C++20 as part of hyphy-3.

Two questions motivated this work. First, how much does directional compositional drift actually break standard FEL? Second, can we model branch-specific compositional drift across hundreds of species without paying the steep computational price of branch-nonstationary phylogenetics?

Let’s start with the first question, because the answer surprised me.

If you test standard FEL against a directional model across empirical alignments—from Drosophila Adh and avian microchromosomes to 869-species vertebrate alignments from the Vertebrate Genomes Project (VGP) and TOGA2—the two methods agree on the selective classification of 96% to 99% of all codon sites.

Why is standard FEL so resilient when its core stationarity assumption is violated?

The reason comes down to two design choices from twenty years ago. First, unlike Goldman–Yang codon models (where substitution rates are proportional to the target codon frequency), FEL uses a Muse–Gaut rate matrix, where rates scale only with the single target nucleotide frequency ($\text{F3}\times 4$). When a lineage experiences a directional push toward GC, that push scales synonymous and non-synonymous changes toward G or C by the exact same factor. Second, FEL estimates a free, site-specific synonymous rate $\alpha_s$ at every site. That $\alpha_s$ parameter acts like a shock absorber: if a site has more or fewer silent substitution opportunities across the tree because of its amino acid identity, $\alpha_s$ soaks up the tree-wide average drift.

So for 96–99% of codons, standard FEL is completely fine. However, at the remaining 1–4% of "boundary" codons, the shock absorber bottoms out—and it fails in two opposite, fascinating ways.

Imagine a clade shifting strongly toward high GC content (say, an avian microchromosome under heavy gBGC):

  • The false purifying trap (riding the tide): Suppose a functionally constrained amino acid site enters the GC-enriching clade carrying an A or T at the third codon position. Because the protein tolerates silent changes and the mutational/gBGC tide favors G and C, that third position rapidly flips to G or C. A stationary model sees a flurry of silent substitutions and infers a huge synonymous rate ($\hat{\alpha}_s \gg 1$). Because $\hat{\alpha}_s$ is the denominator of $\hat{\beta}_s / \hat{\alpha}_s$, inflating $\hat{\alpha}_s$ makes the site look like it is under statistically significant purifying selection—even when the amino acid wasn't under strong constraint.
  • The masked adaptation trap (swimming against the tide): Conversely, suppose a site enters the clade already carrying G or C at the third position. Now silent $\text{GC} \to \text{AT}$ turnover is suppressed by the compositional tide ($\hat{\alpha}_s \ll 1$). Meanwhile, if positive selection drives adaptive amino acid replacements that happen to require $\text{GC} \to \text{AT}$ changes (swimming against the compositional tide), a stationary model underestimates how hard it was to make those changes and misses the diversifying selection signal.

Even worse, splitting conserved sites into "rapid silent turnover" (ancestrally AT-ending) and "silent stasis" (ancestrally GC-ending) artificially blows up the apparent variance in synonymous rates across the gene—what we call Synonymous Rate Variation (SRV).

How do we fix this?

In principle, we know how to write down a non-stationary codon model: give every branch its own target nucleotide frequencies. In practice, that is a computational nightmare for site-level scans. Standard FEL is fast because a time-reversible $61 \times 61$ codon rate matrix $\boldsymbol{Q}_s$ can be diagonalized once per site using symmetric eigendecomposition, and then exponentiated ($\boldsymbol{P}_s(t_b) = \exp(\boldsymbol{Q}_s t_b)$) cheaply across every branch $b$. If every branch has its own non-reversible rate matrix on an 800-species vertebrate tree (~1,600 branches) across 1,000 codons, you have to diagonalize a non-symmetric $61 \times 61$ matrix 1.6 million times per likelihood evaluation. Nobody wants to wait for that.

Other people have tried to sidestep the likelihood problem altogether using heuristic parsimony counting—for instance, the CpG-adjusted method of Suzuki et al. (2009), which weights synonymous and non-synonymous opportunities by fixed mammalian CpG mutation ratios and runs a Binomial test on parsimony counts. But discrete Binomial tests lack statistical power on short or moderate alignments (requiring, for example, 13 non-synonymous changes and 0 synonymous changes at a single arginine codon just to reach $p \le 0.10$), and fixed mammalian CpG weights misfire badly when the bias is avian gBGC or bacterial strand skew rather than mammalian CpG hypermutation.

So: could we build a likelihood model that captures branch-specific directional drift, roots the tree, and still needs only a tiny, constant number of matrix diagonalizations per site?

Our answer is dFEL (Directional Fixed Effects Likelihood), built around a simple mixture trick at the level of transition probabilities.

First, we follow a strict rule: first, do no harm. Before doing anything fancy, dFEL runs a fast Pearson $\chi^2$ homogeneity test on the $N \times 4$ nucleotide counts across taxa (both gene-wide and at third codon positions). If base composition doesn't vary significantly across lineages ($p \ge 0.05$), dFEL sets the number of regimes to $K=1$ and reduces identically to standard FEL. Zero extra parameters, zero discordant calls.

When the alignment is compositionally heterogeneous ($p < 0.05$), dFEL activates three regimes ($K=3$):

  1. AT-enriching ($k=0$): baseline positional nucleotide frequencies tilted toward A and T by a log-odds shift $\psi^{(0)} < 0$.
  2. Neutral ($k=1$): the alignment-wide baseline frequencies ($\psi^{(1)} = 0$).
  3. GC-enriching ($k=2$): baseline positional nucleotide frequencies tilted toward G and C by $\psi^{(2)} > 0$.

Crucially, within each of these three regimes, the instantaneous rate matrix $\boldsymbol{Q}_s^{(k)}$ is still a clean, time-reversible Muse–Gaut matrix. That means at any codon site $s$, we only need three symmetric matrix diagonalizations—one for AT-drift, one for Neutral, and one for GC-drift—regardless of whether the tree has 15 species or 869 species.

How do we get smooth, branch-specific evolution out of just three matrices? Instead of mixing the rate matrices (which would force us to re-diagonalize on every branch), we mix the transition probability matrices $\boldsymbol{P}_s^{(k)}(t_b) = \exp(\boldsymbol{Q}_s^{(k)} t_b)$ on each branch $b$:

$$\boldsymbol{P}_{s, b}(t_b) = w_{b, 0} \boldsymbol{P}_s^{(0)}(t_b) + w_{b, 1} \boldsymbol{P}_s^{(1)}(t_b) + w_{b, 2} \boldsymbol{P}_s^{(2)}(t_b)$$

The weights $(w_{b,0}, w_{b,1}, w_{b,2})$ are assigned smoothly via a Softmax function based on how far that branch's third-position GC content ($\text{GC}_3$) deviates from the background. Branches near the average ride the Neutral matrix; GC-accumulating clades shift smoothly toward the GC-enriching matrix; AT-accumulating clades shift toward the AT-enriching matrix.

Because different branches now use different mixtures of transition matrices while the root sits at the baseline frequencies, Felsenstein's pulley principle no longer applies: the likelihood of the tree now depends on where you place the root. That turns a mathematical nuisance into a biological feature. When given an unrooted tree (--root auto), dFEL scans all $2N-3$ branches during Phase 1 and finds the maximum-likelihood root directly from the directional flow of coding sequence evolution—no outgroup required.

Does it work? It does, across both simulations and a large empirical benchmark suite.

On 1,800 simulated alignments, dFEL keeps false-positive rates nailed to the nominal $5\%$ level under strong directional drift, boosts power to detect positive selection by 4.3 to 8.1 percentage points when adaptive changes swim against the compositional tide (Fig. 1B), and recovers the exact root branch up to 100% of the time on deep trees without an outgroup (Fig. 1C).

When we turned dFEL loose on 87 unique empirical alignments (93 benchmark analyses)—spanning Drosophila Adh, 20 avian macro- and microchromosomal genes, 33 mitochondrial, bacterial, and viral strand-asymmetry loci, and 24 deep vertebrate alignments (464–869 species)—several clear patterns emerged:

  • Massive reduction in phantom Synonymous Rate Variation: Across the sites where FEL and dFEL disagreed in our avian and asymmetry suites, stationary FEL estimated a higher synonymous rate at 95.7% (111/116) of the sites it alone called purifying (Fig. 1D1). For example, at codon 249 of mammalian mitochondrial ND4, rapid silent turnover gave standard FEL an extreme $\hat{\alpha}_s = 13.89$ ($p = 0.013$, purifying); once dFEL accounted for the lineage's compositional drift, $\hat{\alpha}_s$ dropped to $0.78$ ($p = 0.110$, neutral). Across whole genes with strong $\text{GC}_3$ divergence, dFEL cut across-site $\text{Var}(\hat{\alpha}_s)$ by 12.5% to 62.6%—including a 62.6% drop in vertebrate RBP3 and a 41.8% drop in avian AAR2 (Fig. 1D2).
  • Recalibrating avian microchromosomes and deep vertebrate scans: On heterogeneous avian microchromosomes (where gBGC is intense), dFEL tempered 67 inflated purifying calls while unmasking 4 additional positively selected sites (ABCA7, ACKR1, ACOT11; Fig. 2A–B, D). On deep vertebrate trees, dFEL flagged neutral GC-driven parallelisms that fooled standard FEL (such as 15 recurrent $\text{ATT/ATC} \to \text{GTT/GTC}$ isoleucine-to-valine shifts in high-GC mammals at VAMP3 codon 94) while unmasking true positive selection corroborated by MEME (MYOC codon 15).
  • Outgroup-free rooting from coding sequences alone: Without being told anything about species phylogeny, dFEL rooted Drosophila Adh on D. picticornis (capturing the shift into the GC-rich Sophophora clade), rooted mammalian mtDNA on the platypus (Ornithorhynchus anatinus), rooted bacterial rpoB on Candidatus Borrelia tachyglossi, and rooted deep placental/marsupial vertebrate alignments on Afrotheria (Elephantulus edwardii).

This is both a practical tool you can use today and a stepping stone for how we handle non-stationarity across the rest of the HyPhy suite.

Right now, dFEL tilts frequencies along a single $\text{AT} \leftrightarrow \text{GC}$ axis ($\psi^{(k)}$), which captures the dominant compositional tide in gBGC and most coding datasets. Our next steps are to:

  • Add a second tilt axis ($\psi_{\text{GT}}$) to explicitly separate within-class purine/pyrimidine strand skews in mitochondrial and bacterial replicons.
  • Bring this fast three-eigendecomposition transition-matrix mixture into episodic branch-site models like MEME and BUSTED. Unlike site-wide FEL, where a single site-level $\alpha_s$ absorbs most tree-wide drift, branch-site models look for bursts of selection on individual branches—making them much more vulnerable to branch-specific bursts of gBGC or APOBEC deamination.
Try dFEL in HyPhy 3 dFEL is implemented in C++20 in hyphy-3. Run it from the command line (hyphy3 dfel or hyphy3-dfel) or Python (import hyphy3 as hp). Because the $\chi^2$ homogeneity gate returns standard FEL whenever an alignment is stationary, you can use dFEL as a drop-in replacement without losing power on homogeneous genes.
